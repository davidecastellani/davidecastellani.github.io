The Long-Run Effects of Tropical Cyclones on the U.S. Housing Market

Read paper ↗
Abstract: The Long-Run Effects of Tropical Cyclones on the U.S. Housing Market

Housing is a major component of U.S. wealth, yet the long-run impact of tropical cyclones on housing markets remains poorly understood. This paper presents a systematic analysis of all U.S. coastal counties exposed to tropical cyclones from 1992–2021. I find housing values decline by 15% over eight years. Losses are most persistent in counties with little prior cyclone exposure, while high-risk counties rebound. Rising insurance premiums depress housing prices, yet facilitate reconstruction in high-risk areas. A model of heterogeneous risk perception shows how cyclone experience shapes insurance demand and drives divergent long-run housing outcomes.

The Impact of Flooding on Credit Risk: Evidence from a Global Financial Institution

Read paper ↗
Abstract: The Impact of Flooding on Credit Risk: Evidence from a Global Financial Institution

Severe floods significantly worsen firm creditworthiness in developing economies. Using proprietary IFC credit ratings for 3,243 firms (1994–2021) matched to global flood events, I find that a single severe flood deteriorates ratings by 20% within three years, a three-notch downgrade equivalent, primarily through balance sheet weakening, with no significant increase in defaults among IFC’s large borrowers. Repeated exposure reveals partial adaptation at moderate levels (2–4 floods) but renewed deterioration beyond five events. This provides the first causal evidence from emerging markets that climate shocks erode private firm credit quality, with critical implications for development finance as hazard frequency rises.

Beauty and the Blades: The Economic Impact of Wind Turbines

with Gabriele Pinto (Sapienza University of Rome) and Martijn Droes (University of Amsterdam)

Abstract: Beauty and the Blades: The Economic Impact of Wind Turbines

This paper investigates the impact of wind turbines on tourism outcomes. Using data from Italy, we show that the visibility of wind turbines reduces tourist arrivals by up to 20 percent, with the effect peaking approximately seven years after installation. The effect is mainly driven by rural areas. Tourist spending follows a similar pattern, declining by about 15 percent. We further estimate that tourism-related losses offset roughly 35% of the CO2 emission savings resulting from wind turbine installation. These findings highlight the need for policies that mitigate externalities while preserving broader environmental benefits.

The Unintended Consequences of Transparency Laws: Evidence from the Italian Gasoline Market

with Giovanni Mastrobuoni (CCA & UniTO), Andrea Pozzi (EIEF), Simon Martin (DICE), Ragip Kaan Erdemli (University of Barcelona), Marcella Nicolini (University of Pavia)

Abstract: The Unintended Consequences of Transparency Laws: Evidence from the Italian Gasoline Market

We study a Fuel Price Transparency Reform in Italy, according to which stations have to prominently display the previous day’s regional average price next to their own current price. In contrast to the stated objective of the reform, we find no evidence that prices in Italy decreased. Matching stations within Italy, we find that those stations who are located in relatively high-priced stations set relatively higher prices post-reform, suggesting that the competitive pressure of these stations was reduced. The price increase is substantial, corresponding to roughly twice the margin of dealers of branded stations pre-reform.

An empirical socio-economic indicator of country-level food security

with Paolo D’Odorico (UC Berkeley) and Alessandro Tavoni (University of Bologna)

Abstract: An empirical socio-economic indicator of country-level food security

The study of the relationship between poverty and food insecurity across countries remains a challenge because we lack a country-scale economic indicator that captures how poverty reduction is related to access to food. Although empirical evidence for the negative relationship between food security and poverty is abundant at the household level, empirical studies relating undernourishment to income at the country level are less common. Consequently, it is unclear how economic access to food — a pillar of food security — can be quantified in order to facilitate cross-country comparisons.

We propose a global socio-economic index of food security (SEF), defined as the ratio between the per capita income of the least affluent segment of the population and the per capita food expenditure of the whole population. We estimate it for 110 countries in the 2000–2017 period, and test its applicability by evaluating the ability of SEF to explain a country’s performance in feeding its population.

We find evidence of a statistically significant negative relationship between SEF and the Prevalence of Undernourishment (PoU), with SEF explaining a large fraction of the variance of PoU. These results support our hypothesis that prevalence of undernourishment is significantly smaller in countries where the income of the poor is high relative to food expenditure (i.e., with high SEF values).

Following a quantile regression, we find nonlinearities between PoU and SEF, suggesting that it is important to study food insecurity by considering different levels of PoU and economic indicators. In light of the robustness of the empirical evidence, we propose the use of SEF as a quantitative indicator of economic access to food in international development studies. The availability of the underlying data, its cross-country comparability and SEF’s explanatory power suggest that this indicator may complement more granular approaches in informing policymakers about the patterns of food insecurity across time and space.
